# 01 Why a review helps memory, and the principles of order

Work in this order every time: purpose -> what the person will see -> what data that needs -> how to process it.

## 1. Purpose

Notes are written once and rarely reread. Months later only familiarity is left: "I wrote about this", with no content behind it. The review exists so the person can use again what they once worked out: why they looked into it, what they concluded, the reason, and what they changed later.

At review time the author is a stranger to their own notes. Anything that only works if they remember a date, a file name or the evening they wrote it will fail exactly when it is needed.

## 2. What actually moves memory

| Finding | Meaning | What the review does with it |
|---|---|---|
| Retrieval practice | Pulling an answer out strengthens it far more than seeing it again | Every topic gets questions; films ask, wait, then answer |
| Spacing | One exposure fades; returning after growing gaps holds | Questions go into the Review Deck, which schedules them |
| Organisation | Related, ordered material is recalled; a pile of separate facts is not | A skeleton first; every item has a place and a named relation |
| Elaboration | Knowing how and why lets new knowledge attach | Go to the depth the notes reach: method, case, cost, failure |
| Dual coding | A picture plus words gives two routes back | Draw the mechanism; words label it |
| Concreteness | A specific case is kept better than an abstraction | Carry one real case from the notes through a topic |
| Context cues | A cue from the time of learning helps the way back in | One line of context per topic: what the person was doing then |
| Transfer | Recalling only in the original setting is brittle | End with a case the notes do not contain |

Familiarity is not recall. Dates, file names and edit history create familiarity and nothing else, so they are never what a screen is about and never what a question asks for. A note's name and the month it was written may still appear as a small label that says where something comes from (the history in the opening, the sources on the relation map): that is orientation.

A film watched once gives none of the first two rows by itself. That is why the recall pack and its cards come first and the film second.

## 3. The principles of order

Content that is unrelated and unordered is hard to remember; content with relation and order is not. Order is produced on purpose:

1. **Skeleton before content.** Show the few questions the topic answers before any detail. From then on the viewer always knows where they are.
2. **Start simple.** Open with a handful of points. A full relation map at the start is noise; the same map at the end is a summary, because by then every node means something.
3. **One new thing at a time.** Each step adds one element to something already understood.
4. **Fixed places and fixed meanings.** A thing keeps its position; a colour keeps its meaning for the whole film. Position and colour then work as cues.
5. **Few items per level.** About three to five. More than that becomes a list; group it and add a level.
6. **Every relation is named.** A line without the sentence that explains it is decoration.
7. **Same direction throughout.** Left to right, or top to bottom, for cause, sequence and derivation.
8. **Close the loop.** End on the skeleton again, now with the relations visible, and ask the viewer to fill it.

These are reasons, not a layout. A topic that is a story, a comparison or a single mechanism will satisfy them in different ways.

## 4. Acceptance

Two readings by someone who has never seen the notes (a fresh agent is fine); both must pass. How heavy the check is depends on the length tier (`04-production.md`).

- **The words**: reading only the on-screen text in order, can they say what each topic answers, the conclusion and the reason?
- **The pictures**: looking at key stills with the text hidden, can they say what mechanism or relation each picture shows? A still that only announces a subject is a signpost, not content.

And one for memory: can the questions be answered from what was on screen before they were asked?

## 5. What the review is not

- Not a teaching video: no teacher, no curriculum, nothing from outside the notes. It still explains, because the notes themselves explain.
- Not an activity log: when a file was saved and how often is not content.
- Not a showcase: atmosphere is welcome when it costs nothing and never when it shrinks or delays the content.
